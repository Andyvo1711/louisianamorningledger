The reformer machines lined up along the mirrored wall at Magnolia Row Pilates in Metairie can look intimidating to anyone walking past the studio's front window for the first time, a tangle of springs, straps, and sliding carriages that resembles a piece of equipment from a physical therapy clinic more than a fitness class. Studio founder Camille Robicheaux says that first impression is exactly the barrier she has spent three years trying to dismantle.

"So many people tell me they wanted to try Pilates for years but felt too intimidated to walk in," Robicheaux said. "They'd seen classes online that looked advanced and fast, and assumed they'd embarrass themselves. We built this place to prove that's not what it has to be."

## Small Classes, Deliberate Pace

Magnolia Row caps every reformer class at six participants, a deliberate choice that allows instructors to give hands-on adjustments throughout the session rather than calling out cues to a crowded room. New students are required to complete a single private introductory session before joining group classes, giving instructors a chance to assess mobility, past injuries, and general comfort with the equipment before adding the variable of a group setting.

Robicheaux said that structure has been central to the studio's growth, particularly among clients in their fifties, sixties, and beyond who make up a larger share of her clientele than she initially expected when she opened. Many arrive recovering from an injury or managing a chronic condition like osteoporosis or lower back pain, drawn to Pilates specifically because it offers a lower-impact alternative to more strenuous group fitness classes.

"We move slowly on purpose," she said. "Reformer work is not about how fast you can go. It's about control. Once people understand that, the intimidation usually disappears within the first session or two."

## Building Trust Before Building Strength

Instructor training at Magnolia Row places heavy emphasis on communication style, not just technical knowledge of the equipment. Robicheaux said she looks for instructors who can explain a movement in multiple ways, since what clicks for one client's understanding of body mechanics might not land for another.

The studio has also invested in a broader range of spring resistance settings and modified equipment attachments, allowing instructors to adjust difficulty significantly within the same class without singling anyone out. A beginner and an experienced client can work on the same reformer, side by side, at appropriately different resistance levels, without the class ever feeling like it is moving at two different speeds.

## A Ripple Effect Across the Class Roster

The approach appears to be paying off in retention. Robicheaux said roughly seventy percent of clients who complete their introductory private session go on to book at least four additional classes within their first month, a figure she tracks closely as a sign that the studio's low-intimidation model is working as intended.

Several longtime clients have become informal ambassadors, bringing friends and family members who had previously written off Pilates as something only for already-fit, flexible people. Robicheaux said watching that perception shift has been the most satisfying part of running the studio.

"The people who need this the most are often the ones most convinced it's not for them," she said. "If we can get someone through that door once, we can usually keep them."

As Magnolia Row heads into its fourth year, Robicheaux is considering adding a second location elsewhere on the North Shore, though she said any expansion will keep the same small-class philosophy that built the studio's reputation in the first place.
