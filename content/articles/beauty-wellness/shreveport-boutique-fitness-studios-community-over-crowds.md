When Redbird Strength & Conditioning opened in a converted warehouse near downtown Shreveport four years ago, owner Trey Aucoin capped membership at 150 people, a number he arrived at after doing rough math on how many members he could still greet by name. That deliberate limit has become something of a rallying point for the studio, and for a broader shift taking hold among Shreveport's independent fitness studios.

"Big-box gyms compete on price and machine count," Aucoin said. "We can't win that fight, and honestly we don't want to. What we can offer is a coach who knows your name, knows your injury history, and notices if you haven't shown up in two weeks."

## Capacity Limits as a Feature, Not a Flaw

Redbird is not alone in setting hard membership caps. Several boutique studios that have opened across Shreveport in recent years, spanning strength training, kickboxing, and functional fitness formats, have adopted similar limits, treating scarcity as part of the value proposition rather than a growth constraint to overcome.

Aucoin said the cap forces a different kind of business discipline. Rather than chasing constant new member acquisition, the studio focuses heavily on retention, building programming and community events specifically aimed at keeping existing members engaged long-term. When a membership slot does open up, it is often filled from a waiting list built entirely through word of mouth.

## Coaches Who Know Your Story

The staffing model at studios like Redbird looks different from a typical commercial gym as well. Coaches are expected to track individual member progress closely, maintaining notes on past injuries, personal goals, and even life circumstances that might affect a given week's programming. Aucoin said his coaching staff holds a weekly meeting specifically to discuss members who have been struggling, whether physically or simply with motivation, and to plan targeted outreach.

"If someone misses three sessions in a row at a big gym, nobody notices," he said. "Here, three different coaches will have texted them by day four. That's not a coincidence. That's the whole model."

That level of attention has translated into a client base that skews toward long tenure. Aucoin said the average Redbird member has been with the studio for over two years, a retention rate he attributes directly to the relationship-driven coaching approach rather than any particular workout format.

## A Broader Shift in Shreveport's Fitness Landscape

The rise of capped-membership studios in Shreveport reflects a pattern seen in other mid-sized cities, where community-oriented fitness businesses have carved out a loyal niche alongside larger commercial gyms. Local studio owners say the pandemic accelerated the trend, as many people who returned to in-person fitness afterward specifically sought out smaller, more personal environments rather than returning to crowded gym floors.

Several Shreveport studios have also begun hosting regular social events outside of workout hours, from community potlucks to volunteer days, further blurring the line between fitness membership and social club. Aucoin said Redbird's most recent member survey found that nearly half of respondents cited the sense of community, not the workouts themselves, as the primary reason they renewed their membership.

"People can get a good workout in a lot of places," he said. "What's harder to find is a place where people actually notice you, where you'd be missed if you stopped showing up. That's what we're really selling."

As more boutique studios open across the city, Shreveport's fitness scene increasingly looks like a patchwork of small, tightly knit communities rather than a handful of dominant large gyms, a shift local owners expect to continue.
