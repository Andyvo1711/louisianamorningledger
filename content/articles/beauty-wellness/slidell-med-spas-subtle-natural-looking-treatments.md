For much of the past decade, med spa culture nationally leaned toward dramatic transformation, heavier filler, more visible contouring, results built to photograph well under studio lighting. In Slidell, practitioners say that appetite has shifted noticeably over the past year, replaced by a client base actively requesting the opposite: treatments so subtle that friends and coworkers cannot quite identify what changed.

"I probably hear the phrase 'I just want to look like myself but rested' five times a day now," said Dr. Renata Fields, medical director at Heron Point Aesthetics on Gause Boulevard. "That's a completely different conversation than what patients were asking for even three or four years ago."

## Redefining What Success Looks Like

The shift has required a real adjustment in how Heron Point approaches consultations, according to Fields. Rather than starting with a menu of available treatments, appointments now typically begin with an extended conversation about what specifically bothers a patient, followed by a frank discussion of the smallest intervention likely to address it.

Filler volumes have come down markedly, Fields said, with many patients opting for a fraction of what was once considered a standard treatment amount. Neuromodulator injections aimed at fine lines have similarly trended toward lighter dosing that preserves natural facial movement rather than eliminating expression entirely.

"We spend a lot of time now talking people out of doing too much in one visit," Fields said. "The instinct used to be, if a little is good, more must be better. We're actively pushing back against that instinct."

## Skin Quality Over Structural Change

Alongside the shift toward subtlety, Slidell med spas report growing demand for treatments focused on skin quality rather than structural alteration, including microneedling, chemical peels calibrated for Louisiana's humid climate, and laser treatments targeting sun damage and texture rather than volume or shape. Fields said this category now makes up a larger share of Heron Point's bookings than injectables for the first time since the practice opened.

That trend has also changed staffing needs. Heron Point has added additional licensed estheticians to handle the growing skin-quality service line, working alongside the practice's nurse injectors rather than treating the two service categories as entirely separate business lines.

## Consultations Get Longer, Not Shorter

Perhaps the clearest sign of the shift is appointment length. Initial consultations at Heron Point now regularly run forty-five minutes, roughly double what Fields said was standard several years ago, driven largely by more detailed conversations about realistic expectations and a patient's specific goals rather than a generic treatment menu walkthrough.

Fields said she has also become more comfortable telling prospective patients that a treatment they are asking about is not the right fit for their goals, a stance she credits with building longer-term trust even when it means turning down revenue in a given visit.

"If someone leaves looking overdone, that reflects on us, not just on them," she said. "Word travels fast in a community this size. Doing conservative, natural work well has become our actual reputation, and honestly, that reputation is worth more than any single treatment."

## A Regional Shift, Not Just a Local One

Fields said colleagues at other Louisiana practices, from Mandeville to Hammond, have described a similar pattern among their own patient bases, suggesting the shift toward subtlety reflects a broader regional and generational change in aesthetic preferences rather than something unique to Slidell alone.

Whatever the cause, Fields said the change has been a welcome one from a clinical standpoint. "Conservative treatment plans are just better medicine," she said. "It's nice when what patients want and what's actually best for them line up this closely."
