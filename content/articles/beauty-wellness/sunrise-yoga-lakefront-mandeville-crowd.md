Before most of Mandeville has finished its coffee, a crowd of mats is already unrolled along the lakefront near the harbor, facing east toward the water as the sky shifts from gray to pink. What began three years ago as a casual gathering of a dozen neighbors has grown into a standing Saturday tradition drawing upward of sixty people most weeks, rain schedules permitting.

The sessions are led by Josephine Trahan, a longtime instructor who started teaching the class after noticing how many people in her regular studio sessions mentioned wishing they could practice outdoors, closer to the lake they lived near but rarely slowed down to appreciate.

"I set up one Saturday with a borrowed speaker and told a few students to bring their mats," Trahan said. "The next week there were twice as many people. I didn't plan for it to become what it's become."

## Practice Shaped by the Setting

Unlike a typical studio class, the lakefront sessions are built around the natural rhythm of sunrise, starting in near-darkness with slow, grounding movement before building toward a more active flow as the light fully arrives. Trahan said she adjusts her sequencing weekly based on the season, keeping poses gentler during the hottest months when even early morning heat can make more strenuous practice uncomfortable, and layering in longer holds during the cooler months when the lakefront breeze makes extended stillness more pleasant.

The setting itself has become part of the appeal. Regulars describe the sound of water against the seawall and the occasional passing pelican as a kind of built-in meditation aid that no studio playlist can replicate. Trahan has leaned into that, deliberately keeping her cues minimal during certain segments of class to let the ambient sounds of the lake take over.

## From a Handful of Neighbors to a Fixture

The class's growth has not been entirely without challenges. Trahan has had to adapt her teaching style considerably as the group expanded from an intimate, easily correctable dozen to a crowd where many students in back rows cannot always see her demonstrations clearly. She now uses a small portable microphone and relies more heavily on verbal cueing than visual demonstration, a shift she said improved her teaching overall.

"Being forced to describe a pose clearly with words, instead of just showing it, actually made me a better instructor," she said. "I had gotten a little lazy relying on demonstration."

The class remains donation-based, with proceeds rotating periodically to different local causes, which Trahan credits with keeping the atmosphere welcoming rather than commercial. New attendees are common most weeks, often visitors who heard about the class from a Mandeville relative or stumbled across it while walking the lakefront path.

## A Model Other Communities Are Watching

Word of the lakefront class's steady growth has reached instructors in nearby Covington and Slidell, several of whom have experimented with their own outdoor sunrise sessions in public parks, though none has yet matched the scale of the original Mandeville gathering.

Trahan said she has no plans to formalize the class into a paid studio offering, preferring to keep it as accessible as it was on that first borrowed-speaker Saturday. "The whole point was that anyone could show up," she said. "If I started charging real studio rates for something on public land, it would stop being what it is."

As Mandeville continues to grow, the Saturday sunrise gathering has become something of a fixture, a quiet, communal start to the weekend that shows no signs of losing momentum.
