Anyone who has stepped outside in Louisiana between May and September knows the feeling: the air itself seems to have weight. That heavy, humid climate does more than fog up glasses and wilt hairdos. It fundamentally changes what skin needs, and local estheticians say most popular skincare advice simply was not written with this climate in mind.

"So much of what circulates online comes from places with dry winters and mild summers," said Colette Arceneaux, lead esthetician at Marigold Skin Studio in Metairie. "We tell clients constantly: your skin here is not doing the same thing skin in Colorado is doing. You have to build a routine for where you actually live."

## Why Humidity Changes Everything

In humid conditions, skin tends to produce more oil as it compensates for the moisture-laden air, which can lead to clogged pores and breakouts even in people who consider their skin naturally dry. At the same time, sweat mixes with sunscreen, makeup, and pollution throughout the day, creating a film that can irritate sensitive skin if it is not properly cleansed each evening.

Arceneaux said the biggest mistake she sees is clients layering heavy, occlusive moisturizers meant for arid climates onto already oil-prone skin. "You end up trapping moisture that has nowhere to go," she said. "That's a recipe for congestion, especially along the jawline and forehead."

Instead, many Louisiana estheticians now recommend lightweight, gel-based moisturizers and a double-cleanse routine in the evening: a gentle oil or balm cleanser to break down sunscreen and sweat, followed by a foaming or gel cleanser to remove any remaining residue. Morning routines lean toward antioxidant serums and a broad-spectrum sunscreen with a matte or semi-matte finish that can hold up under humidity without sliding off by midday.

## Sunscreen Habits Get an Upgrade

Sun protection has become a bigger conversation in local skincare circles, not because clients are unaware they need it, but because so many people abandon their sunscreen by early afternoon once it wears off in the heat. Estheticians are increasingly recommending powder or spray reapplication options that can go over makeup without disturbing it, along with wide-brim hats for anyone spending extended time outdoors.

"Reapplication is the piece everyone skips," Arceneaux said. "We're trying to make it easier so it actually happens, because a morning-only sunscreen routine in this climate just isn't enough."

## Seasonal Adjustments Matter More Here

Unlike climates with a dramatic seasonal shift, Louisiana's version is subtler but still significant. Winter here still brings lower humidity and occasional cold snaps that can leave skin feeling tight, particularly for anyone who spent the summer relying entirely on lightweight products. Estheticians recommend building in a richer moisturizer for the handful of genuinely cool weeks each year, then transitioning back to gel formulas as spring humidity returns.

Facial treatments have adapted too. Many studios now offer humidity-focused facials that combine a deeper pore-clearing step with a lightweight hydration boost, aiming to address the congestion that heavy air can cause without over-drying skin in the process.

Arceneaux said the shift in thinking has been gradual but steady over the past several years, as more clients arrive already skeptical of routines borrowed wholesale from national trends. "People are paying attention to their own skin now instead of just copying what works somewhere else," she said. "That's the biggest change I've seen. They're asking what actually works here, in this air, in this heat."
