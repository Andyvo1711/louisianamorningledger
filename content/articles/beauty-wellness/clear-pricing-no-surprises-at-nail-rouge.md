Price is rarely the real problem on its own. What tends to upset a client is a total that arrives without warning, built from charges she was not told about in advance. This report on Nail Rouge is a clear example: the individual prices may be ordinary, but the way the extras appeared left the customer feeling, in her words, that the place was a rip off. The fix is simple, and it is all about communication.

## When the Total Feels Bigger Than Expected

The client listed what she paid: a full set of white tips for $45, and a polish change on her toes for $25. Numbers like these can be perfectly reasonable, but they land very differently when a client is not sure going in what each service will cost. A visible, easy-to-read price menu, and a quick heads-up on the total before starting, lets a customer decide with confidence instead of doing the math at the register and feeling surprised.

## Charges That Weren't Explained First

The frustration really came from the add-ons. On what she understood to be a simple polish change, she was charged extra because old gel polish had to be removed first, and she was also charged $2 for paying with her card. Neither charge is unusual on its own, gel removal does take time, and card fees are common, but both landed as surprises. An add-on a client does not see coming feels like a penalty, even when it is standard. Mentioning "there is a small charge to take the old gel off first" and "we add a $2 fee for card payments" before the service, or posting them plainly at the desk, changes them from a shock into a simple, fair choice the client makes with open eyes.

## Media & Customer Experience Perspective

The encouraging part is that nothing here requires lowering prices, only making them clear. First, post a simple, visible price list and confirm the expected total before work begins, so no number is a surprise at checkout. Second, disclose every add-on up front: gel removal, extra length, designs, and especially any card-payment fee should be stated before the client sits down, not added silently at the end. Many salons choose to absorb a small card fee entirely, since the goodwill is usually worth more than $2. Third, frame extras as choices, not penalties, so the client always feels in control of what she is paying for. Handled this way, the same prices this customer paid would have felt fair and transparent, and she would have left as a returning client rather than a frustrated one.
