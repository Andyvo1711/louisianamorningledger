Twenty minutes outside downtown Houma, past the last stretch of commercial buildings and onto a quiet bayou-side road, a cluster of raised cypress cottages makes up the entirety of Egret Bend Retreat. There is no restaurant, no gift shop, and, by design, almost no cell signal. Founder Lucille Prejean built the retreat five years ago around a single guiding principle: guests come here to do less, not more.

"Everyone arrives a little anxious about what they're going to do with all that unstructured time," Prejean said. "By Sunday morning, most people don't want to leave. That's usually how I know it worked."

## A Weekend With Almost No Schedule

Unlike many wellness retreats built around packed itineraries, Egret Bend's weekend program is intentionally sparse. Guests choose from a small handful of optional offerings, a morning guided meditation session, an evening reflection circle, a slow paddle along the bayou at sunset, but nothing is mandatory, and long stretches of the schedule are left entirely open.

Prejean said that structure, or lack of it, was a direct response to what she observed in guests during her earlier years running more traditional retreat programming elsewhere. "People would leave a jam-packed retreat weekend more exhausted than when they arrived," she said. "I wanted to build something where rest was actually the point, not just a word on the brochure."

Phones are not confiscated, but guests are strongly encouraged to leave them in their cottages during common gathering times, and the retreat's limited signal in the surrounding marsh does much of that work naturally. Prejean said most guests report, somewhat to their own surprise, that they stop reaching for their phones by the second day.

## Built Around the Bayou

The retreat's setting plays a central role in its programming. Cottages are elevated on stilts overlooking a quiet stretch of water, and much of the reflection work Prejean guides draws directly on the rhythms of the surrounding marsh, tide changes, bird activity, the particular quality of light at dusk, as a kind of ambient teaching tool for slowing down.

Evening sessions often take place on a shared dock, where Prejean leads a loosely structured discussion inviting guests to talk about what brought them to the retreat in the first place, without requiring anyone to share more than they are comfortable with. She said the format has evolved over years of trial and error into something that consistently produces genuine, unforced conversation rather than performative wellness talk.

## Meals as Part of the Slowness

Meals at Egret Bend are prepared on-site by a small kitchen staff using ingredients sourced largely from local growers and fishers, served family-style at long communal tables rather than individually plated. Prejean said the shared-meal format was one of the retreat's most requested elements in guest feedback, with many visitors saying the unhurried mealtime conversations became an unexpected highlight of their stay.

"We're not trying to be a fancy culinary destination," she said. "We just want food that feels honest to this place, eaten slowly, with people you might not have known forty-eight hours earlier."

## A Small Operation by Choice

Egret Bend intentionally caps each weekend at sixteen guests, a number Prejean has resisted pressure to expand even as demand has grown enough to keep the retreat booked several months in advance. She said the small scale is essential to the experience the retreat is trying to create.

"The second this gets big, it stops being what people are coming here for," she said. "I'd rather stay small and keep it real than grow and lose the whole point."
