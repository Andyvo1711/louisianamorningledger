Etienne Robichaux can trace his family's shrimp boats back four generations along the bayous of Terrebonne Parish, but he is the first Robichaux to build a processing plant instead of just a dock. Bayou Terrebonne Seafood Co., which he founded in 2017, now buys catch from more than thirty independent shrimping and oystering families across the parish, processes it at a facility outside Houma, and sells directly to restaurants and consumers across the Gulf South.

"My grandfather used to say the shrimp business would eat itself alive competing against foreign imports," Robichaux said, standing on the dock where his family has unloaded boats since the 1960s. "He wasn't wrong about the threat. He just didn't live to see that the answer was for us to control more of the supply chain ourselves instead of just handing the boat's catch off to whoever offered the lowest price that week."

## Fighting Imports With a Direct-to-Consumer Model

Louisiana's wild-caught shrimp industry has spent years contending with cheaper farm-raised imports that have depressed dockside prices for independent fishing families. Robichaux's response was to build Bayou Terrebonne Seafood Co. as a vertically integrated brand, buying catch at above-market dockside prices, processing and flash-freezing it at his facility, and marketing it directly to consumers and restaurants as authentic, traceable wild-caught Louisiana shrimp.

The company's packaging includes the name of the boat and, where possible, the family that caught each batch, a transparency feature Robichaux says resonates strongly with consumers increasingly skeptical of unlabeled imported seafood. Bayou Terrebonne now ships frozen product to restaurants as far away as Nashville and Atlanta, alongside a growing local retail presence in Houma-area grocery stores.

"Etienne pays us better than the traditional dock buyers ever did, and he actually tells our story instead of just taking our catch," said Delphine Cheramie, who has sold her family's shrimp boat catch to Bayou Terrebonne since 2019. "For the first time in years, I feel like the business is fighting for us instead of just squeezing us."

## Rebuilding After Storm Losses

The company suffered a major setback in 2021 when hurricane damage destroyed a portion of its original processing facility, forcing a costly rebuild that Robichaux financed partly through a state disaster recovery loan and partly by taking on a minority investment from a New Orleans-based food distributor. That investment, while diluting his ownership stake, gave Bayou Terrebonne the working capital to modernize its cold storage and expand processing capacity by nearly 60 percent.

"I hesitated a long time before taking outside money," Robichaux admitted. "But I needed the plant rebuilt right, not rebuilt cheap and vulnerable to the next storm. Sometimes protecting something you built alone means you can't keep doing everything alone."

## Advocating for Louisiana's Fishing Families

Beyond his own company, Robichaux has become an active voice in state discussions over seafood labeling requirements, pushing for stricter rules requiring restaurants to disclose when shrimp served is imported rather than Gulf-caught. He has testified before a state legislative committee on the issue twice in the past two years.

"This isn't really about one company's bottom line," Robichaux said. "It's about whether the working waterfront in Terrebonne Parish still exists in twenty years, or whether it becomes something people just remember from old photographs. I've got two kids. I'd like there to still be boats for them to inherit if they want them."
