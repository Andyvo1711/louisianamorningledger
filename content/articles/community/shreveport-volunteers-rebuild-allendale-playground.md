By mid-morning on a warm Saturday in Shreveport's Allendale neighborhood, the sound of power drills and laughter drowned out the usual quiet of Betty Virginia-adjacent side streets. More than 100 volunteers, ranging from teenagers on summer break to retirees who grew up in the neighborhood decades ago, had gathered to build a new playground on the site of a lot that had sat mostly bare since the old equipment was condemned and removed years earlier.

By late afternoon, swings hung from freshly assembled frames, a climbing structure stood where weeds had once grown through cracked concrete, and a mural painted by local high school art students brightened a retaining wall along the lot's edge. Organizers estimated the group completed roughly ninety percent of the build in a single day, with a smaller crew returning the following weekend to finish landscaping and safety surfacing.

## A Neighborhood Effort Years in the Making

The project traces back to a series of neighborhood association meetings where residents repeatedly raised the lack of safe outdoor play space for children in Allendale. Unlike some newer subdivisions on the outskirts of Shreveport, the historic neighborhood has few dedicated recreational areas, and the previous playground equipment had been unusable for so long that many younger children in the area had never known it any other way.

"My kids used to ask why there was a fence around a broken slide," said Yolanda Pierce, a mother of three who has lived in Allendale for over a decade and helped coordinate volunteer sign-ups for the build. "Today they got to help paint part of the mural that's going to be right next to where they play. That means something."

Funding for the materials came through a combination of a national playground-building nonprofit's matching grant program, a local church's community outreach budget, and a modest fundraising drive that included a neighborhood chili cook-off held earlier in the year. Organizers say the mixed funding approach was necessary because grant programs alone rarely cover the full cost of both materials and the safety-certified installation the project required.

## Building More Than Equipment

Volunteer coordinators intentionally structured the build day to involve as many neighborhood residents as possible, rather than relying solely on outside contractors. Teams were split by task, with some volunteers assembling structural components under the guidance of a certified playground builder while others handled landscaping, mulch spreading, and the mural project running along the lot's perimeter fence.

Organizers say that structure was deliberate. Community build days of this kind, they note, tend to create a stronger sense of ownership over a shared space than simply having equipment installed by an outside crew with no community involvement. Several volunteers who worked on the build said they planned to return regularly to help maintain the space, checking mulch levels and reporting any equipment issues to the neighborhood association.

Shreveport's parks department has committed to routine inspections of the new equipment going forward, and neighborhood leaders say they hope the project can serve as a model for other underserved pockets of the city still waiting for updated recreational space. A waiting list of interested neighborhoods has already begun to form, according to organizers involved in coordinating the build.

For now, Allendale families are simply enjoying having somewhere new to bring their children after school. On the Sunday following the build, neighbors reported a steady stream of kids testing out the new slide, proof that a single determined Saturday of community labor could reshape a corner of the neighborhood that had been overlooked for years.
