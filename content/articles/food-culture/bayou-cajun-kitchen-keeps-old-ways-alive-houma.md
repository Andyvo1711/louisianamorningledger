Follow the bayou road south out of Houma far enough, past the marinas and the shrimp docks, and the pavement eventually narrows to a single lane running alongside water on both sides. That's where you'll find LeBlanc's Bayou Table, a low wooden building with a tin roof and a wood-fired smokestack that's been sending up thin curls of smoke over the marsh grass for as long as most locals can remember.

Inside, the kitchen runs almost entirely on cast iron and open flame, a setup that would look more at home in a photograph from decades past than in a working restaurant kitchen today. The family that runs it has resisted, mostly out of principle, the pull toward gas ranges and stainless steel that has reshaped kitchens across much of Cajun country.

## Cooking the Way It Was Always Done

"My father cooked over wood, and his father cooked over wood," said the current owner, tending a pot of smothered turtle sauce piquante that had been going since before dawn. "You lose something when you switch to gas. The heat's different. The smoke gets into the food different. I'm not interested in losing that."

The kitchen's signature dish, a sauce piquante built on whatever the family's own trotlines and duck blinds happened to produce that week, changes with the seasons in a way that most restaurant menus no longer do. In late winter it might be venison. Come spring, it leans toward soft-shell crab pulled straight from traps set along the bayou behind the building. The one constant is the dark, slow-built roux, coaxed along in a blackened pot that never fully leaves the stovetop.

Rice, too, gets treated with a reverence that outsiders sometimes find surprising. The family still uses a variety grown by a cousin's small farm near Gueydan, insisting the local rice holds up differently under a heavy, spicy sauce than the varieties sold in most grocery stores.

## Passing Down What Can't Be Written Down

What makes LeBlanc's Bayou Table unusual isn't just the cooking method — it's how deliberately the family has worked to pass the knowledge down without letting it calcify into a written recipe. The owner's teenage daughter has spent the past two summers working the wood-fired stove under close supervision, learning to judge roux color by sight and smell rather than by a timer, the same way her father learned from his.

"You can't really write down how dark is dark enough," the daughter said, adjusting a split log under one of the pots. "You just watch it enough times that you know. My dad still checks my roux sometimes, even though I've been doing this for two summers."

The restaurant draws a mix of longtime locals, out-of-town hunters and fishermen passing through Houma, and a slowly growing number of food travelers who've heard about the wood-fired kitchen through word of mouth rather than any marketing effort. There's no sign visible from the main highway, no social media account, and no reservation system — just a hand-painted sign at the turnoff and a line that sometimes stretches out the door on weekend evenings.

For a region where so much of the food culture has become shorthand for tourism — crawfish towers and gift-shop hot sauce — LeBlanc's Bayou Table stands as a reminder that the older, slower version of Cajun cooking is still out there, tended by families who never stopped doing it the hard way, one split log and one blackened pot at a time.
