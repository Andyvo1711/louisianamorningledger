The recipe card was so worn that the flour amount had faded almost entirely off the page, leaving only a smudge of pencil and a coffee ring where a measurement used to be. That's the state the owner of Ardoin's Sweet Shop found her grandmother's king cake recipe in, tucked inside a metal box in a closet in north Shreveport, three years after her grandmother had passed.

"I had to call my aunt and basically reconstruct it from memory," she said, standing behind a display case lined with braided, cinnamon-swirled dough dusted in purple, green, and gold sugar. "Between the two of us, we got it close. Close enough that when my aunt tasted the first test batch, she cried a little."

## Piecing Together a Lost Recipe

Shreveport's king cake tradition has never carried quite the same citywide fanfare as New Orleans', but families across the city still treat the pastry as an essential marker of the weeks leading into Mardi Gras season. Ardoin's Sweet Shop, a narrow storefront that opened only two years ago on a quiet stretch near the Highland neighborhood, has quickly become known for chasing that older, homemade version of the cake rather than the mass-produced style found in many grocery store bakeries.

The bakery's version leans notably less sweet than what many newcomers expect, with a brioche-style dough enriched with real butter and a cinnamon filling that stays modest rather than syrupy. The glaze, too, follows the grandmother's original instructions almost exactly: a thin, barely-there icing rather than the thick fondant coating found on many modern versions.

"My grandmother used to say a king cake shouldn't fight the coffee," the owner said. "It's supposed to go with your coffee, not compete with it."

Reconstructing the recipe required more than guesswork. The owner spent months testing batches in her home kitchen before opening the shop, adjusting yeast quantities and proofing times based on her aunt's recollections and a handful of surviving family photographs that showed the cake's shape and color from decades-old holiday gatherings.

## A Recipe Worth Preserving

Word of the bakery's origin story spread quickly through word of mouth, and by the shop's second king cake season, lines were forming before opening time on weekend mornings throughout January and February. Regulars now request specific variations that have become shop traditions in their own right, including a version filled with locally made cane syrup instead of the standard cinnamon filling, a nod to the family's rural Louisiana roots outside the city.

The bakery has also become something of a gathering point for other Shreveport families hoping to preserve their own recipes. The owner has started hosting informal recipe-sharing evenings once a month, where neighbors bring in worn cookbooks, handwritten cards, and half-remembered instructions from relatives who have passed, hoping for help testing and reconstructing them the same way she did with her grandmother's cake.

"So many of these recipes only exist in somebody's memory now," she said. "If we don't write them down, test them, get them right again, they just disappear. I got lucky that my aunt remembered as much as she did. Not everybody's going to be that lucky."

For a bakery that started as one woman's attempt to hold onto a piece of her grandmother, Ardoin's Sweet Shop has become something larger for the Highland neighborhood: proof that a recipe worn nearly illegible on an old card can still find its way back to a full display case, purple and green sugar catching the morning light, exactly the way someone once intended it to.
