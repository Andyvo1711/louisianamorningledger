The smell hit before the sun even cleared the tree line: cayenne, garlic, and boiling crab boil steam curling up from a propane burner set behind Pelletier's Crawfish Shack on the edge of Lafayette. Most years, folks in Acadiana wait until Mardi Gras season to see the big pots come out in earnest. This year, thanks to an unusually mild winter and warm rains through the Atchafalaya Basin, the crawfish came early, and nobody around here is complaining.

"We had customers lined up before we even had the second pot going," said the shack's owner, who took over the roadside stand from his uncle a decade ago. "Usually that's a February crowd. This year it's January and they're already asking for extra corn."

## Why the Season Arrived Sooner

Crawfish farmers across the Atchafalaya basin and the rice-and-crawfish rotation fields near Rayne and Church Point say the early harvest comes down to water temperature. Crawfish burrow into muddy fields during the hot, dry months, then emerge once the fields flood and temperatures cool into the ideal range. A wet December followed by a stretch of unseasonably warm days in early January created exactly the conditions crawfish like best, and processors say pond yields were running ahead of last year's pace by nearly three weeks.

That has ripple effects across the food economy. Restaurants that typically wait until Ash Wednesday to build their boil menus started advertising specials the first week of the new year. At Broussard's Bayou Table in Breaux Bridge, a Cajun institution known locally simply as "the Table," owners added a Friday all-you-can-eat crawfish special a full month ahead of their usual schedule, and it sold out its first three weeks running.

Prices, predictably, told their own story. Early season crawfish tend to run smaller and pricier per pound before farms hit peak production, and this year was no exception, with live crawfish opening around six dollars a pound at roadside stands before easing down as more ponds came online. Buyers who know the rhythms of the season snapped up early batches anyway, treating the first boil of the year as something worth paying a premium for.

## A Ritual That Never Gets Old

For many Acadiana families, the first crawfish boil of the season is less about the food itself and more about what it signals: porch weather, backyard gatherings, and the slow build toward Mardi Gras. Newspaper spread across long folding tables, a five-gallon bucket for shells, cold drinks in a washtub of ice — the choreography barely changes from year to year, even when the calendar does.

At a boil hosted behind a modest brick house off Pinhook Road, three generations gathered around steaming trays of crawfish, potatoes, corn, and whole heads of garlic. The grandmother of the house, who has hosted the same gathering every crawfish season for over thirty years, said an early start just means an early excuse to bring everyone together.

"You don't need a reason to boil crawfish in Louisiana," she said, cracking a tail with practiced hands. "But if the season wants to start in January instead of March, we're not going to argue with it."

Local seafood markets are bracing for what could be one of the longer crawfish seasons in recent memory, with farmers projecting good yields clear through June if rainfall stays steady. For a state that treats crawfish season less as a calendar date and more as a state of mind, the early arrival is being read as good news all around — more Fridays for boils, more weekends for family, and more reasons to gather around a newspaper-covered table before the year gets away from anyone.
