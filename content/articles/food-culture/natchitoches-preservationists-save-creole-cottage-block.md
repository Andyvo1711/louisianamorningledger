Along a quiet block just off the Cane River in Natchitoches, four Creole cottages stand shoulder to shoulder, their steep hipped roofs and deep porches marking them as survivors of a building style that once defined much of the town's oldest neighborhoods. Two of the four sat vacant for years, slowly losing ground to termite damage and weather, until a small group of local preservationists decided the block was worth fighting for.

"You drive through parts of this town and you don't realize how much has already been lost," said the founder of the informal preservation group, a retired schoolteacher who grew up two streets over from the cottage block. "These four houses are some of the last of their kind still standing this close together. Once they're gone, that context is gone with them."

## A Building Style Worth Fighting For

Creole cottages, built low to the ground with steeply pitched roofs designed to shed heavy rain and porches meant to catch river breezes, once lined much of Natchitoches' older neighborhoods, reflecting the French and Creole heritage that shaped the town long before Louisiana became a state. Many were built by free people of color and Creole families in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, using construction techniques suited to the region's heat, humidity, and flooding along the Cane River.

Over the past several decades, many of these structures across the region have been demolished, replaced, or altered so extensively that little of their original character survives. The block near the river represents one of the last clusters where the cottages remain close enough together to be understood as a group rather than isolated curiosities.

The preservation group has spent the past two years cataloguing the block's architectural details, tracking down old photographs, and interviewing longtime residents about the families who lived in the cottages going back multiple generations. One of the two occupied cottages is still owned by descendants of the family that built it, a detail the group considers central to their preservation case.

## Building Support, One Conversation at a Time

Preservation efforts in smaller Louisiana towns often struggle for funding and attention compared to larger cities with bigger tourism budgets, and Natchitoches has been no exception. The group has relied largely on small local fundraisers, a handful of state historic preservation grants, and volunteer labor from residents willing to spend weekends clearing brush, patching roofs, and stabilizing foundations well enough to buy the buildings more time.

"We're not trying to turn this into a museum nobody can touch," the group's founder said. "We want people living in these houses again, using them the way they were meant to be used. A house that's lived in gets taken care of. A house that sits empty just falls apart faster."

That approach has started to win over some initially skeptical neighbors, including a young couple who recently purchased and began renovating one of the vacant cottages after attending a preservation group meeting out of curiosity. They've committed to restoring the original porch structure and roofline while updating the interior for modern living, a compromise the preservation group has held up as a model for how the remaining block might be saved.

Local historians say the stakes extend beyond the four buildings themselves. The block, they argue, offers a rare, tangible link to a period of Natchitoches history that shaped the town's identity long before it became known for its bridges, its historic downtown, and its holiday lights. Losing the cottages, one preservationist said, would mean losing one of the last physical spaces in town where that history can still be seen, touched, and lived in, rather than simply read about in a historical marker along the riverbank.
