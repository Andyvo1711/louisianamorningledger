Before church lets out, before the football pregame shows start, before most of Baton Rouge has finished its coffee, a certain kind of Sunday morning sound is already underway in kitchens across the city: the slow, patient scrape of a wooden spoon against the bottom of a cast iron pot. It's roux season, and in this city, roux season is every Sunday of the year.

The gumbo pot at Doucet Family Kitchen, a modest storefront tucked into a strip of shops off Perkins Road, has not gone empty on a Sunday in eleven years. The family that runs it treats the weekly pot less as a menu item and more as an obligation, one that gets passed down the same way a family Bible might.

## Roux Before Anything Else

Ask any home cook in this city what makes a gumbo theirs, and the conversation starts and ends with the roux. Some families swear by a roux the color of milk chocolate, patient and nutty. Others push it darker, into the territory locals call "brick" or "mahogany," a shade that takes the better part of an hour of constant stirring to reach without scorching.

At Doucet Family Kitchen, the roux gets made in a pot large enough to feed the extended family that shows up every week, plus whichever neighbors happen to wander by hungry. The current keeper of the recipe learned the technique standing on a step stool next to her grandmother, who insisted a good roux could not be rushed, timed, or delegated to anyone in a hurry.

"You cannot text and stir roux at the same time," she said, laughing, while nudging a pot of dark roux with a flat wooden paddle worn smooth at the edges. "My grandmother used to say the roux tells you when it's ready. You just have to be patient enough to listen."

## More Than a Meal

What makes Baton Rouge's Sunday gumbo tradition remarkable isn't any single recipe — it's how consistently it shows up across neighborhoods, income levels, and backgrounds. Drive through the Garden District on a Sunday afternoon and the smell of simmering stock, bell pepper, celery, and onion drifts from open windows. Head out toward the university area and you'll find the same ritual playing out in small apartments, with roommates taking turns at the stove because somebody's mother taught them the recipe over a phone call.

Local seafood shops report a predictable Sunday-morning rush for andouille, smoked sausage, and fresh Gulf shrimp, timed almost exactly to when families start their pots. A butcher counter near Government Street said regulars have been coming in at the same hour, requesting the same cuts, for so many consecutive Sundays that staff know the order before it's spoken.

For newer residents and transplants to the city, the tradition can feel like an invitation waiting to be extended. A young family that moved to Baton Rouge for work two years ago said their next-door neighbors showed up on their first Sunday in the new house with a covered pot and a stack of bowls, no explanation needed beyond, "This is just what we do around here."

That instinct — to feed first, explain later — runs through the whole tradition. Gumbo, more than almost any other dish in Louisiana cooking, exists as much for the gathering as for the food itself. The roux takes an hour. The stock takes longer. But the real ingredient, longtime cooks will tell you, is simply showing up, pot in hand, every single Sunday, whether there's a special occasion or not.

As one Baton Rouge home cook put it while ladling out bowls for a table of neighbors and cousins: "Gumbo doesn't need an occasion. Sunday is the occasion."
