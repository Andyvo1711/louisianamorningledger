Loyola University New Orleans has introduced a new mentorship program designed to support recent graduates of its College of Education during their first two years in the classroom, a period widely recognized as the most likely time for new teachers to leave the profession altogether. The program pairs early-career teachers with experienced educators from partner schools across the New Orleans area, offering structured check-ins, classroom observation feedback, and a confidential outlet for discussing the challenges of a first teaching job.

National research on teacher attrition has repeatedly identified the first few years of a teaching career as the most fragile, with a significant share of new teachers leaving the profession before their fifth year. Local school leaders in New Orleans have echoed those concerns, noting that turnover among early-career teachers creates instability for students and adds recurring recruitment costs for schools already working within tight budgets.

## More Than Just Classroom Observation

Loyola's mentorship model goes beyond simple classroom visits. Mentors, many of whom are veteran teachers with a decade or more of experience, are trained to provide instructional coaching alongside emotional and logistical support, recognizing that new teachers often struggle as much with time management, parent communication, and classroom culture as they do with lesson planning itself.

Program coordinators within the College of Education describe the mentorship relationship as intentionally distinct from formal school-based evaluation, giving new teachers a space to admit struggles or ask questions without fear that doing so will affect a performance review. That distinction, coordinators say, is central to the program's design, since new teachers are often reluctant to voice difficulties to supervisors responsible for their formal evaluations.

## Building Long-Term Retention in New Orleans Schools

The mentorship initiative grew out of conversations between Loyola faculty and administrators at several New Orleans charter and public schools who reported struggling to retain teachers hired straight out of the university's education program. Rather than treating teacher preparation as complete at graduation, Loyola's approach extends the university's involvement into a new graduate's first years on the job, treating early-career support as a continuation of teacher training rather than a separate concern for individual schools to manage on their own.

Mentors participating in the program receive a modest stipend and training on effective coaching techniques, ensuring the relationship offers genuine instructional value rather than informal encouragement alone. Meetings between mentors and mentees occur regularly throughout the school year, with additional touchpoints built in around particularly stressful periods, such as the weeks surrounding standardized testing or the start of a new semester.

University officials say early feedback from participating schools has been positive, with several principals reporting that mentored teachers appear more confident managing classroom challenges compared to previous cohorts of new hires who lacked structured support. The program's design also allows for data collection on retention outcomes, giving Loyola a way to measure whether mentored teachers remain in the profession, and in New Orleans specifically, at higher rates than those without access to similar support.

As New Orleans schools continue to navigate staffing challenges common to urban districts nationwide, Loyola's mentorship program represents an attempt by a local university to take direct responsibility for outcomes beyond graduation day, treating the transition from student to full-time educator as a critical phase worth continued institutional investment.
