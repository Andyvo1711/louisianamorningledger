Southern University has established a new scholarship fund specifically targeted at first-generation college students from the Baton Rouge area, aiming to close financial gaps that federal Pell Grants and existing state aid programs often leave unaddressed. The fund, administered through the university's financial aid office, will provide need-based awards to incoming freshmen who are the first in their immediate family to pursue a four-year degree.

As one of the nation's historically Black universities, Southern has long served as a critical access point to higher education for students from communities where college attendance rates have historically lagged. University officials say the new scholarship fund reflects a recognition that even with federal and state aid, many first-generation students still face financial barriers, including costs for textbooks, housing deposits, and technology, that can derail their path to graduation.

## Filling Gaps Existing Aid Doesn't Cover

Financial aid administrators at Southern point out that a full ride is rare even for students who qualify for maximum Pell Grant awards, since tuition, fees, housing, and living expenses often exceed what federal and state programs combined are able to cover. The new scholarship fund is designed to be flexible, allowing recipients to apply funds toward whichever expense category creates the greatest barrier to their continued enrollment, rather than restricting the award to tuition alone.

The program also includes a mentorship component, pairing scholarship recipients with upperclassmen or recent graduates who were themselves first-generation students. Organizers say this peer support structure is intended to address a challenge that often goes unmentioned in conversations about college access: first-generation students frequently lack a family member who can help them navigate unfamiliar systems, from registering for classes to understanding financial aid renewal deadlines.

## A Response to Persistent Retention Challenges

National data has consistently shown that first-generation college students, on average, complete degrees at lower rates than their peers whose parents attended college, often due to a combination of financial strain and unfamiliarity with the institutional processes that shape a successful college experience. Southern's scholarship initiative is explicitly framed by administrators as a retention tool as much as a recruitment one, aiming not just to get students in the door but to keep them enrolled through graduation.

University leaders have emphasized that the scholarship fund was built with input from current students who identified specific financial pressure points during their own college experience, from the cost of parking permits to unexpected fees during registration periods. That feedback shaped decisions about how flexible the scholarship disbursement process would be, allowing students facing an unplanned expense to request support rather than waiting for a fixed disbursement schedule.

The fund launches with support from private donors and university foundation resources, and officials say they hope to grow it over time through continued fundraising efforts aimed at alumni and regional business partners. Baton Rouge community organizations have also expressed interest in contributing, viewing the scholarship as a direct investment in local students who might otherwise struggle to complete a degree.

For many Baton Rouge families, Southern University represents an accessible and affordable path into higher education, and administrators say the new scholarship fund is meant to reinforce that role at a moment when the overall cost of college continues to rise nationally. By focusing specifically on first-generation students, the university hopes to make measurable progress on a persistent equity gap in college completion rates.
